## 1. What this site is for

One job: when a manufacturing leader, a staffing partner, or a prospective client lands here — usually because Alex sent the link, Amy Schrimpf's office shared his resume, or John Schrimpf forwarded an article — the site confirms, within 30 seconds, that Alex Finnarn is a seasoned software consultant whose experience transfers to their world, who is deliberately building into industrial automation in the Dayton region, and who is easy to contact.

It is a credibility instrument, not a playground, not a blog-with-a-homepage, not a portfolio of experiments.

## 3. Positioning

**The line (use verbatim until replaced by something better):**
> Alex Finnarn — Senior Software Consultant, Troy, Ohio
> Thirteen years building and modernizing business-critical software systems. Now bringing that experience to industrial automation and the Dayton region's manufacturers.

**Supporting proof strip (numbers, always):** 13+ years · 20 production sites migrated without downtime drama · a giving platform processing $5.4M a year · federal and higher-education clients (VA, CMS, University of Colorado, University of Denver) · Python, SQL, and integration work throughout.

**The honest bridge clause** — never hide the crossing, frame it as trajectory:
> "My career has been spent on the software that businesses run on: legacy systems that can't go down, integrations that have to be right, data people make decisions from. I'm now applying that same discipline to the systems factories run on — studying the Ignition platform, learning the plant floor's language, and writing about the crossing as I go."

Credibility rule: claim the 13 years loudly, claim automation as direction and study — never as finished expertise he doesn't yet have. Manufacturers can smell an inflated controls résumé; the honest version is the differentiator.

## 4. Voice — professional, no snark

The standard: a senior consultant's letter, not a developer's blog.

- Plain sentences. Specific nouns. Numbers over adjectives. Warm and direct.
- Confidence without bravado: state what was done and what it produced; let the reader conclude he's good.
- Respect for the reader's world: their systems keep people employed and product shipping; write like it matters.
- **Retired, permanently:** snark and takedowns; dev in-jokes and slang ("whatever-end developer," "neckbeard" humor, Scots-Irish wit as a brand); self-deprecating career humor; superhero/consultant-pitch theatrics; placeholder or jokey content of any kind (no "Foop," no unfinished experiments on public pages); writing about recruiters, job hunting, or career ambivalence.
- The test for every paragraph: *Would I be comfortable with Amy Schrimpf reading this aloud to her board?* If not, rewrite or cut.

**Explicitly not on the site:** the Ink/visual-novel experiment, fractal or art experiments, a projects page of half-finished apps, a blogroll of abandoned platforms, jokes in footers or 404 pages, analytics-vanity or follower counts.

## 6. Design direction

- **Feel:** the website equivalent of a well-run plant office — clean, ordered, everything labeled, nothing decorative that doesn't earn its place. Think engineering firm or regional bank, not startup, not terminal window.
- **Layout:** generous whitespace, one column of substance, wide readable text measure, clear headings. No hero animations, no parallax, no dark-mode hacker aesthetic.
- **Type:** a professional sans (system stack or one quality family), large enough to read without zooming. Headlines say things; no wordplay headlines.
- **Color:** near-black ink on white, one restrained accent (deep steel blue or a muted industrial orange), used sparingly for links and the proof strip.
- **Photography/images:** minimal. His portrait on About; otherwise none or abstract diagrams drawn for a specific article. No stock photos of laptops or handshakes.
- **Performance and craft as a signal:** static site (the existing Astro stack is fine — the stack was never the problem), loads instantly, works on a phone in a plant manager's truck, accessible contrast and focus states. For this audience, a fast, sober site *is* the portfolio.
- **Details that read as care:** correct title/meta on every page ("Alex Finnarn — Senior Software Consultant, Troy, Ohio" pattern), a real favicon, working links, no lorem ipsum anywhere, a footer © line with his name spelled right.

## 8. Acceptance tests (the site is "done" when all pass)

- **The Amy test:** a manufacturers' association president lands on the homepage cold and can say, in one sentence, who he is and why a member might call him.
- **The John test:** a staffing partner can forward any page to a client without editing or apologizing for anything on it.
- **The plant-manager test:** every page answers "has he done work like this, at stakes like mine?" with at least one number.
- **The 30-second test:** positioning, proof, and contact are all visible without scrolling on a phone.
- **The six-month test:** nothing on the site is a joke, a placeholder, or a claim that will embarrass him when the Ignition credential lands and the story gets more serious.
